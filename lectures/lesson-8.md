---

### Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson, I should be able to:

1. Establish the integrated diagnosis and explain why IDH mutation alone is insufficient.
2. Distinguish grade 2 from grade 3 without inventing a grade 4 oligodendroglioma category.
3. Choose among observation, vorasidenib, and RT plus chemotherapy for a grade 2 tumor.
4. Defend RT plus PCV as the evidence-based standard for a fit patient who needs definitive treatment.
5. Explain when TMZ is a reasonable substitute and why TMZ alone is not equivalent.
6. Prescribe standard PCV and vorasidenib, including monitoring, holds, reductions, and major interactions.
7. Interpret surveillance MRI using the appropriate nonenhancing-tumor framework.

---

### 1. Start with the integrated diagnosis

Under WHO CNS5, **oligodendroglioma requires both**:

- A pathogenic **IDH1 or IDH2 mutation**, and
- **Whole-arm 1p/19q codeletion**.

Neither oligodendroglial morphology nor isolated loss of 1p or 19q is enough. A tumor with an IDH mutation but intact 1p/19q is an astrocytoma, not an oligodendroglioma.

#### Practical molecular workup

| Test | What it establishes or supports | Clinical pearl |
|---|---|---|
| IDH1 R132H immunohistochemistry | Detects the common IDH1 mutation | If negative in an appropriate patient, sequence IDH1 and IDH2; a negative stain does not prove IDH-wildtype disease |
| 1p/19q testing | Must show whole-arm codeletion | Prefer a method able to distinguish whole-arm from focal loss; interpret FISH cautiously in unusual or discordant cases |
| ATRX | Usually retained | ATRX loss argues toward astrocytoma and should trigger reconciliation of the molecular results |
| p53 | Usually not diffusely overexpressed | A strong diffuse mutant pattern raises concern for astrocytic lineage |
| TERT promoter | Common, supportive | Not independently diagnostic without IDH mutation and whole-arm codeletion |
| CIC and FUBP1 | Common lineage-associated alterations | Supportive but not required |
| CDKN2A/B | Can be prognostic | Unlike IDH-mutant astrocytoma, homozygous deletion does **not** create a WHO grade 4 oligodendroglioma |
| DNA methylation profiling | Resolves selected discordant cases | Useful when histology, ATRX/p53, and copy-number findings do not fit together |

The classic histology—uniform round nuclei, perinuclear halos, branching capillaries, and calcification—is helpful but cannot replace the molecular definition.

#### Grade 2 versus grade 3

WHO CNS5 recognizes only **grades 2 and 3** for oligodendroglioma. Grade 3 is assigned by anaplasia, including increased cellularity and mitotic activity, often with microvascular proliferation and/or necrosis. There is no single universally accepted mitotic cutoff, so grading retains an element of integrated pathologic judgment.

Important consequences:

- Necrosis does not convert the tumor into glioblastoma.
- There is no CNS WHO grade 4 oligodendroglioma category.
- Sampling matters: a small biopsy can undergrade a heterogeneous tumor.
- Marked enhancement, rapid growth, high perfusion, or diffusion restriction should prompt careful sampling and pathology review but do not independently determine WHO grade.

### 2. Initial clinical and imaging assessment

#### Before and after surgery

- MRI brain with and without contrast using a standardized glioma protocol: 3D T1 pre/post contrast, T2, FLAIR, diffusion; perfusion and spectroscopy when helpful.
- Maximal safe resection is preferred when feasible. Extent of resection is prognostic, but preservation of language, motor, executive, and network function takes priority.
- Obtain early postoperative MRI, ideally within **24–48 hours**, to document residual nonenhancing and enhancing disease before postoperative change obscures the result.
- Document KPS/ECOG status, cognition, neurologic deficits, seizure burden, antiseizure medication, steroid requirement, reproductive goals, hearing, neuropathy, constipation risk, marrow reserve, renal and hepatic function.
- Spine MRI and CSF are not routine without symptoms or radiographic concern for dissemination.
- Germline testing is not routine solely because an oligodendroglioma is present; use the personal and family history.

#### Baseline laboratory assessment before systemic treatment

- CBC with differential and platelets
- CMP, including AST, ALT, alkaline phosphatase, total bilirubin, creatinine/eGFR and electrolytes
- Pregnancy testing when relevant
- Medication and supplement reconciliation
- For PCV: assess baseline neuropathy and bowel function; consider pulmonary function if there is lung disease or concern for lomustine toxicity
- For vorasidenib: document baseline blood chemistry and liver tests and review CYP interactions and tobacco use

### 3. The central grade 2 decision: can definitive chemoradiation safely be deferred?

The correct question is not simply “young or old?” It is: **Does this patient need definitive RT plus chemotherapy now, or can we safely delay it?**

#### Factors favoring observation or an IDH inhibitor strategy

- Gross-total or near-total resection
- Small, nonenhancing residual tumor
- Minimal or no neurologic deficit
- Seizures controlled
- Slow or absent interval growth
- Good performance status
- No clinical need for rapid cytoreduction
- Strong desire to delay the late effects of RT and alkylating chemotherapy

#### Factors favoring RT plus chemotherapy now

- Symptomatic or progressive residual disease
- Large tumor burden, mass effect, or meaningful neurologic deficit
- Rapid serial growth
- Significant or increasing enhancement concerning for aggressive biology
- Grade 3 pathology
- Need for more reliable and durable cytoreduction
- Progression during observation or on vorasidenib
- A patient who prefers the treatment with the longest mature survival evidence

Age 40 and extent of resection defined the “high-risk” RTOG 9802 population, but modern decisions should not be reduced to that binary rule. Molecular diagnosis, tumor volume, growth rate, eloquent location, symptoms, functional status, life goals, and treatment toxicity all matter.

### 4. Grade 2 treatment pathways

#### Pathway A: active surveillance

Observation remains guideline-concordant after surgery in selected favorable patients. It is an active management strategy, not an absence of care.

Use it when:

- The tumor is grade 2 and definitively molecularly classified.
- The patient is clinically stable.
- Immediate RT/chemotherapy is not required.
- Serial MRI can be obtained reliably.

Do not wait for a fixed percentage of growth if the patient develops a new neurologic deficit, uncontrolled seizures, meaningful acceleration, enhancement, or mass effect. A second resection can be considered for diagnostic clarification, cytoreduction, seizure control, or updated molecular material.

#### Pathway B: vorasidenib

**Guideline position:** The April 2025 ASCO–SNO update conditionally recommends vorasidenib for grade 2 IDH-mutant oligodendroglioma when, after one or more surgeries, RT and chemotherapy have been or can be deferred. The FDA indication includes grade 2 oligodendroglioma with a susceptible IDH1/2 mutation after biopsy, subtotal resection, or gross-total resection.

**Best evidence-matched patient:** A patient resembling INDIGO—grade 2 IDH-mutant glioma, surgery as the only previous antitumor treatment, measurable predominantly nonenhancing disease, KPS at least 80, no immediate need for RT/chemotherapy, and no tumor-related glucocorticoid requirement.

The FDA label is broader than the trial: it does not require measurable residual disease or restrict initiation to 1–5 years after surgery. The farther the patient is from the INDIGO population—for example, bulky enhancing disease or a need for rapid shrinkage—the more explicit the uncertainty should be.

**Do not use vorasidenib as an evidence-based substitute for definitive treatment in:**

- CNS WHO grade 3 oligodendroglioma
- A clinically aggressive tumor requiring prompt cytoreduction
- A patient previously treated with RT or cytotoxic chemotherapy, outside a trial or carefully justified off-label setting
- A patient progressing rapidly on an IDH inhibitor

#### Pathway C: RT followed by PCV

For a grade 2 oligodendroglioma that requires definitive postoperative treatment, **RT plus PCV has the strongest mature survival evidence**. ASCO–SNO recommends RT plus PCV; TMZ is a reasonable alternative when PCV toxicity is a concern.

Typical RT dose:

- **50.4–54 Gy** in **1.8-Gy fractions** for grade 2 disease.
- RTOG 9802 used **54 Gy in 30 fractions**, followed by six cycles of PCV.

### 5. Grade 3 treatment pathway

For a fit adult with newly diagnosed grade 3 oligodendroglioma, standard therapy is:

1. Maximal safe resection, then
2. **RT 59.4 Gy in 33 fractions**, then
3. **Adjuvant PCV**, typically six 6-week cycles using the conventional schedule below.

This sequence mirrors EORTC 26951 and is commonly preferred because its regimen maps directly to the positive adjuvant-PCV trial. RTOG 9402 used an intensified neoadjuvant PCV regimen before RT; it proves the principle that adding PCV improves long-term outcome in codeleted tumors but is not the routine contemporary schedule.

Temozolomide can replace PCV when marrow reserve, neuropathy, comorbidity, frailty, logistics, or patient preference make PCV impractical. The patient should understand that tolerability is better, but mature randomized evidence demonstrating equivalence to PCV is not available.

### 6. PCV versus TMZ: the question an attending will ask

| Question | PCV | TMZ |
|---|---|---|
| Survival evidence in codeleted oligodendroglioma | Mature randomized OS benefit with RT in EORTC 26951 and RTOG 9402; supportive grade 2 data from RTOG 9802 | No completed phase 3 trial proving equivalence to RT–PCV |
| Guideline status | Preferred evidence-based combination with RT | Reasonable alternative when PCV toxicity is a concern |
| Administration | Six-week multidrug cycles; oral lomustine/procarbazine plus IV vincristine | Oral; commonly 5 days every 28 days, with or without concurrent RT depending on regimen |
| Major toxicity | Delayed/prolonged myelosuppression, nausea, fatigue, neuropathy, constipation, hepatotoxicity, pulmonary toxicity | Myelosuppression, nausea, fatigue, lymphopenia, opportunistic infection risk during prolonged daily dosing, hepatotoxicity |
| Practical burden | Higher; dose delays and incomplete delivery are common | Lower |
| Key uncertainty | Which components and duration are essential; vincristine contribution is debated | Whether RT–TMZ provides the same very long-term survival as RT–PCV |

**Bottom line:** Do not say “TMZ is the same as PCV.” Say: “PCV has the mature survival evidence; TMZ is a guideline-accepted, better-tolerated alternative when PCV is not suitable, while CODEL is designed to answer the comparative question.”

#### Why TMZ alone is not an acceptable shortcut

The original CODEL design included RT alone, RT with concurrent/adjuvant TMZ, and TMZ alone. In the small early cohort, TMZ alone produced significantly shorter PFS than the RT-containing arms: HR **3.12**. The trial was redesigned and now compares RT plus PCV with RT plus TMZ.

Therefore, for a patient who needs definitive therapy, **TMZ alone should not be presented as equivalent to an RT-containing regimen**.

### 7. Exact systemic regimens

#### Conventional adjuvant PCV: commonly used after RT

One cycle is **42 days**:

| Drug | Dose and schedule |
|---|---|
| Lomustine (CCNU) | **110 mg/m² orally once on day 1** |
| Procarbazine | **60 mg/m² orally daily on days 8–21** |
| Vincristine | **1.4 mg/m² IV on days 8 and 29; cap at 2 mg** |
| Planned duration | **Six cycles**, if tolerated |

This is the RTOG 9802-style schedule and is consistent with conventional adjuvant PCV used after RT. Institutions may modify or omit vincristine for clinically significant neuropathy; that is pragmatic practice, not a randomized proof that “PC” is equivalent to PCV.

**Historical intensified RTOG 9402 schedule:** lomustine 130 mg/m² day 1; procarbazine 75 mg/m²/day days 8–21; vincristine 1.4 mg/m² IV days 8 and 29 without a 2-mg cap, for four cycles before RT. Do not accidentally order this historical intensified regimen when intending standard adjuvant PCV.

#### Temozolomide alternative

Common institutional options include:

- RT followed by adjuvant TMZ: **150 mg/m² orally on days 1–5 of cycle 1**, then **200 mg/m² on days 1–5 every 28 days** if tolerated.
- RT with concurrent TMZ: **75 mg/m² orally daily throughout RT**, followed by adjuvant TMZ 150–200 mg/m² on days 1–5 every 28 days.

The optimal sequencing and number of TMZ cycles in oligodendroglioma are not established by a completed comparative phase 3 trial. Six to 12 adjuvant cycles are used in practice depending on the chosen protocol; do not present one duration as proven equivalent to six cycles of PCV.

### 8. PCV monitoring, supportive care, holds, and reductions

#### Before each cycle

- CBC with differential and platelets
- AST, ALT, alkaline phosphatase, total bilirubin, creatinine/eGFR and electrolytes
- Review fever/infection, bleeding, nausea, oral intake, constipation, neuropathy, gait, falls, pulmonary symptoms, medications and supplements
- Confirm that oral drug instructions are understood; dispensing errors with lomustine can be catastrophic

Because lomustine produces a **late and sometimes prolonged marrow nadir**, many centers obtain CBCs at least around days 29 and 42 and more often after cytopenia or during early cycles. Exact weekly versus interval monitoring is institution-dependent.

#### A conservative cycle-start framework

Ontario Health’s regimen monograph advises not starting a new cycle until:

- **ANC at least 1.5 × 10⁹/L**
- **Platelets at least 100 × 10⁹/L**
- Organ toxicity has recovered to **grade 2 or lower**

eviQ notes that selected clinically well patients may be treated at lower counts under local protocols, but emphasizes the delayed nadir. Follow the institutional order set rather than blending thresholds from different protocols.

#### Supported dose-modification examples

| Toxicity | Practical response supported by oncology protocols |
|---|---|
| Febrile neutropenia, grade 4 neutropenia, thrombocytopenic bleeding, or grade 3+ thrombocytopenia | Delay until recovery; reduce subsequent lomustine and procarbazine to approximately 75% |
| Pretreatment ANC 0.5 to <1.0 × 10⁹/L or platelets 50 to <75 × 10⁹/L | Delay until recovery |
| Pretreatment ANC <0.5 × 10⁹/L or platelets <50 × 10⁹/L | Delay; eviQ recommends 25% reduction of subsequent lomustine and procarbazine |
| Grade 2 neuropathy present at the next cycle | Reduce vincristine; if persistent, reduce further or omit |
| Grade 3–4 neuropathy | Omit vincristine; some protocols discontinue the regimen for severe neurotoxicity |
| Severe hepatic, renal, pulmonary, or other organ toxicity | Hold and obtain pharmacy/protocol-specific adjustment; do not improvise a universal reduction |

These are protocol-level guides, not a replacement for the local PCV order set. PCV dose modification is less standardized than vorasidenib labeling.

#### Supportive care and interactions

- Prescribe an antiemetic before lomustine and during procarbazine as needed.
- Start an active constipation plan, particularly with vincristine and antiemetics.
- Procarbazine has monoamine-oxidase-inhibiting properties. Review serotonergic drugs, sympathomimetics, alcohol, and high-tyramine foods with oncology pharmacy; institutional restrictions vary.
- Avoid live vaccines during clinically significant immunosuppression.
- Discuss fertility preservation before RT and alkylating chemotherapy.
- Use effective contraception and avoid pregnancy during therapy; verify each drug’s required post-treatment duration in the current label.
- PJP prophylaxis is not routinely required for adjuvant PCV alone, but reassess with prolonged corticosteroids or profound lymphopenia.
- G-CSF is not automatically required with every cycle; consider secondary prophylaxis after febrile neutropenia or according to individualized risk and the institutional protocol.

### 9. Vorasidenib prescribing: exact practical guidance

#### Dose

- Adults: **40 mg orally once daily**, continuously.
- Take with or without food, at approximately the same time each day.
- If a dose is missed by more than 6 hours, skip it; do not double.
- If vomiting occurs after a dose, do not replace it; take the next scheduled dose the following day.
- Continue until progression or unacceptable toxicity; there is no established fixed number of cycles.

#### Baseline and ongoing monitoring

- Check blood chemistry and liver tests before treatment.
- Monitor **AST, ALT, GGT, alkaline phosphatase, and total bilirubin every 2 weeks for the first 2 months**, then **monthly for the first 2 years**, and as clinically indicated.
- Obtain MRI at a consistent interval, commonly every 3–4 months early in treatment.
- Track seizures, cognition, function, fatigue, diarrhea, nausea, musculoskeletal symptoms, and adherence.

#### Adult dose-reduction levels

1. Starting dose: **40 mg once daily**
2. First reduction: **20 mg once daily**
3. Second reduction: **10 mg once daily**
4. Permanently discontinue if 10 mg once daily is not tolerated.

#### Hepatotoxicity algorithm from the June 2026 FDA label

| Laboratory toxicity | Action |
|---|---|
| Grade 1 ALT/AST >ULN to 3 × ULN, without bilirubin >2 × ULN | Continue current dose; monitor liver tests weekly until recovery to below grade 1 |
| First grade 2 ALT/AST >3–5 × ULN, without bilirubin >2 × ULN | Hold until grade 1 or baseline. If recovery is within 28 days, resume same dose; if >28 days, resume one dose level lower |
| Recurrent grade 2 | Hold until grade 1/baseline, then resume one dose level lower |
| First grade 3 ALT/AST >5–20 × ULN, without bilirubin >2 × ULN | Hold. If recovery within 28 days, resume one dose level lower; if not recovered within 28 days, permanently discontinue |
| Recurrent grade 3 | Permanently discontinue |
| ALT/AST >3–20 × ULN with bilirubin >2 × ULN | First event: hold until grade 1/baseline, then resume reduced; recurrence: permanently discontinue |
| Grade 4 ALT/AST >20 × ULN | Permanently discontinue |
| Other first grade 3 adverse reaction | Hold until grade 1/baseline, then resume reduced; permanently discontinue if recurrent |
| Other grade 4 adverse reaction | Permanently discontinue |

The 2026 label includes rare serious postmarketing acute hepatic toxicity, including hepatic failure, hepatic necrosis, and autoimmune hepatitis. A new supplement, multivitamin, herbal product, alcohol exposure, or interacting medication should be reviewed rather than assumed benign.

#### Major interactions and reproductive counseling

- Avoid strong or moderate **CYP1A2 inhibitors**; if a moderate inhibitor cannot be avoided, monitor closely for toxicity.
- Avoid moderate **CYP1A2 inducers** and **tobacco smoking**, which may lower exposure.
- Vorasidenib can reduce concentrations of sensitive CYP3A substrates.
- It can reduce hormonal-contraceptive effectiveness; use effective **nonhormonal contraception** as directed by the label.
- Counsel regarding embryo-fetal toxicity, lactation restrictions, and possible male and female infertility.

### 10. Radiation planning principles

| Setting | Common dose | Evidence context |
|---|---|---|
| Grade 2 requiring definitive therapy | **50.4–54 Gy in 28–30 fractions** | RTOG 9802 used 54 Gy/30 |
| Grade 3 | **59.4 Gy in 33 fractions** | EORTC 26951 and RTOG 9402 used 59.4 Gy/33 |

Modern focal RT targets the postoperative cavity and residual T2/FLAIR abnormality with protocol-defined clinical and planning margins. It is not whole-brain RT. Contouring, margin size, and whether to include edema are radiation-oncology and protocol dependent.

Discuss:

- Neurocognitive and vascular late effects
- Fatigue, alopecia, skin reaction, and edema
- Endocrine effects for tumors near the hypothalamic-pituitary axis
- Fertility and gonadal exposure where relevant
- Proton therapy when normal-tissue sparing is meaningfully improved; there is no universal requirement that oligodendroglioma be treated with protons

### 11. What the landmark trials actually proved

#### RTOG 9802: high-risk grade 2 glioma

**Design:** Phase 3 trial of RT alone versus RT followed by six cycles of PCV. “High risk” meant age 40 or older with any resection extent, or younger than 40 with subtotal resection/biopsy. Histology predated modern molecular classification.

**Treatment:** RT 54 Gy/30 fractions; conventional adjuvant PCV.

**Results:**

- Median PFS: **10.4 versus 4.0 years**, HR 0.50.
- Median OS: **13.3 versus 7.8 years**, HR 0.59.
- The survival benefit emerged late.

**Limitation:** Not restricted to molecularly defined oligodendroglioma; post hoc molecular analyses support benefit in IDH-mutant groups. It establishes RT–PCV as a grade 2 diffuse-glioma standard when treatment is required, but it does not answer observation versus immediate therapy in every favorable patient.

#### EORTC 26951: grade 3 oligodendroglial tumors

**Design:** RT alone versus RT followed by six cycles of PCV; molecular testing was largely retrospective.

**Treatment:** RT 59.4 Gy/33 fractions; adjuvant PCV.

**Final long-term result:** In the 1p/19q-codeleted subset, median OS was approximately **14.2 years with PCV versus 9.3 years without PCV**; HR 0.60, with wide confidence intervals because the subgroup was small.

**Limitation:** Enrolled by older histologic criteria, and the codeleted analysis was retrospective. Nevertheless, the magnitude and durability align with RTOG 9402.

#### RTOG 9402: intensified PCV before RT

**Design:** Four cycles of intensified PCV followed by RT versus RT alone for anaplastic oligodendroglial tumors.

**Final codeleted result:** Median OS **13.2 versus 7.3 years**; HR 0.61. Approximately 37% of codeleted patients assigned PCV–RT were alive at 20 years.

**Limitation:** The molecular subgroup analysis was not the original primary design, and the intensified pre-RT regimen is more toxic than contemporary conventional adjuvant PCV. Use it to support the treatment principle, not to copy the regimen automatically.

#### INDIGO: vorasidenib for grade 2 IDH-mutant glioma

**Design:** International phase 3, double-blind trial; 331 patients age 12 or older randomized to vorasidenib 40 mg daily or placebo. Surgery was the only previous treatment. Patients had measurable nonenhancing grade 2 IDH-mutant glioma, KPS at least 80, and no immediate need for RT/chemotherapy; the most recent surgery was 1–5 years before randomization.

**Original primary results:**

- Median PFS: **27.7 versus 11.1 months**; HR 0.39.
- Time to next intervention: HR **0.26**; median not reached versus 17.8 months.

**Later findings:** Vorasidenib reduced volumetric growth and seizure rate without an observed adverse effect on measured health-related quality of life or neurocognition during reported follow-up. Updated analyses continue to show durable PFS and delay of next intervention, but crossover complicates OS interpretation.

**Limitations:** No OS benefit has been established; the trial did not compare vorasidenib with RT–PCV; it excluded patients who required immediate definitive therapy and did not study grade 3 disease.

#### CODEL: the unresolved PCV-versus-TMZ question

The original small cohort showed inferior PFS with TMZ alone compared with RT-containing arms. The redesigned international phase 3 trial compares **RT plus PCV versus RT plus TMZ**. Until mature results are available, PCV retains the stronger survival evidence.

### 12. MRI surveillance and response assessment

#### Practical surveillance intervals

Current NCCN guidance individualizes frequency by treatment and risk. A practical framework is:

| Clinical state | MRI brain with and without contrast |
|---|---|
| After surgery only for grade 2 | As often as every **3–4 months** until progression; selected patients with gross-total resection and prolonged stability may later extend cautiously |
| During vorasidenib | Commonly every **3–4 months** initially, maintaining a consistent protocol |
| After RT plus chemotherapy for oligodendroglioma | At least every **6–9 months** until progression; many clinicians image more frequently early after treatment or for grade 3 disease |
| After RT alone or chemotherapy alone | As often as every **3–4 months for 5 years**, then generally no longer than every 6 months until progression |
| New symptoms, escalating seizures, steroid need, or equivocal growth | Image earlier; consider short-interval MRI |

Obtain a post-RT MRI for a new baseline, commonly **2–8 weeks after RT**. Long-term surveillance is necessary because recurrence can occur after many years.

#### RANO 2.0 for predominantly nonenhancing IDH-mutant glioma

- Measure the **T2/FLAIR** component, not only enhancement.
- Measurable disease has clearly defined margins and two perpendicular diameters of at least **10 mm** on a slice, visible on at least two slices.
- Use consistent acquisition, slice thickness, steroid documentation, and clinical assessment.
- Consider both enhancing and nonenhancing components in a mixed tumor.
- New or increased enhancement is concerning but may reflect treatment effect, seizure, ischemia, or inflammation.
- Within 12 weeks after RT, suspected progression generally requires confirmation with repeat MRI or unequivocal pathology when the patient is clinically stable.
- Volumetric analysis is useful for slowly growing nonenhancing tumors, but routine thresholds and software are not fully standardized.

When conventional MRI is indeterminate, perfusion MRI, spectroscopy, diffusion, or amino-acid PET can support—but not replace—multidisciplinary interpretation and serial follow-up.

### 13. Management at recurrence

First confirm that the change represents tumor progression rather than treatment effect, seizure-related change, infarct, infection, or another process. Review the entire treatment history and the interval since RT and alkylator exposure.

Options include:

- Clinical trial, especially before using an unproven targeted combination
- Repeat resection for accessible focal disease, symptom relief, cytoreduction, or updated tissue
- RT if the patient has not previously received it
- Carefully selected reirradiation after prior RT
- TMZ after prior PCV, or a nitrosourea/PCV-based approach after prior TMZ, depending on marrow reserve and prior toxicity
- Bevacizumab for symptomatic edema or radiation necrosis; it is not proven to extend OS in oligodendroglioma
- An IDH-inhibitor trial or other molecularly directed trial
- Symptom-directed care, rehabilitation, seizure management, and goals-of-care support

Vorasidenib’s established evidence is in previously untreated grade 2 disease after surgery only. Using it after RT/chemotherapy or in grade 3 recurrence goes beyond INDIGO and should be framed as off-label or trial-based.

### 14. Attending-style clinical case 1

#### Case

A 34-year-old right-handed woman has a first generalized seizure. She undergoes near-total resection of a left frontal, nonenhancing tumor. Pathology shows oligodendroglioma, IDH1 R132H-mutant and whole-arm 1p/19q-codeleted, CNS WHO grade 2. Postoperative MRI shows a 1.8-cm measurable FLAIR residual along the deep margin. KPS is 100, language is intact, seizures are controlled on levetiracetam, and serial MRI over six months shows slow growth without enhancement. She wants to delay RT because of long-term cognitive concerns.

#### Model attending-level answer

I would first confirm the integrated diagnosis and review the postoperative and interval MRIs volumetrically if available. She has grade 2 disease, excellent performance status, controlled symptoms, small nonenhancing residual tumor, and no immediate need for cytoreduction. Observation and vorasidenib are both reasonable; definitive RT plus PCV is also effective but need not be forced immediately in a favorable patient.

I would discuss vorasidenib 40 mg orally daily because she resembles the INDIGO population: surgery only, measurable nonenhancing residual disease, KPS at least 80, and the ability to defer RT/chemotherapy. I would explain that INDIGO improved PFS and delayed the next intervention but has not shown an OS advantage and did not compare the drug with RT–PCV. Treatment postpones rather than eliminates the possible future need for chemoradiation.

Before treatment I would check CBC/CMP and complete liver tests, reconcile medications and supplements, assess smoking and CYP1A2 interactions, and discuss fertility and nonhormonal contraception. I would monitor liver tests every two weeks for two months, monthly thereafter for two years, and MRI approximately every 3–4 months. New enhancement, faster growth, uncontrolled seizures, neurologic decline, or mass effect would trigger reconsideration of surgery and/or RT plus chemotherapy.

**What would lose points:** Saying she must receive RT–PCV solely because there is residual tumor; calling vorasidenib curative; quoting the FDA indication without explaining how the trial population was narrower; or omitting liver monitoring.

### 15. Attending-style clinical case 2

#### Case

A 56-year-old man undergoes subtotal resection of a large right frontal enhancing tumor. Pathology confirms oligodendroglioma, IDH2-mutant and whole-arm 1p/19q-codeleted, CNS WHO grade 3. KPS is 90. He has no baseline neuropathy, CBC and organ function are adequate, and he asks whether oral TMZ would be “just as good and easier.”

#### Model attending-level answer

I would recommend focal RT to 59.4 Gy in 33 fractions followed by six cycles of conventional adjuvant PCV if he accepts and can tolerate it. He has grade 3 disease with substantial residual tumor and therefore is not an evidence-based candidate for observation or vorasidenib in place of definitive therapy.

The rationale is the very long-term survival evidence from EORTC 26951 and RTOG 9402. In molecularly codeleted tumors, adding PCV to RT reduced the risk of death by about 40%, with median OS around 13–14 years and a meaningful fraction alive at 20 years. I would not prescribe the intensified historical RTOG 9402 doses. I would use the conventional six-week schedule: lomustine 110 mg/m² day 1, procarbazine 60 mg/m²/day days 8–21, and vincristine 1.4 mg/m² IV, capped at 2 mg, on days 8 and 29, for six cycles if tolerated.

TMZ is easier and is a guideline-accepted alternative if PCV toxicity is a major concern, but I would tell him honestly that it has not been proven equivalent to PCV in a completed randomized phase 3 trial. TMZ alone should not replace RT; the original CODEL cohort had inferior PFS with TMZ alone. If he chooses TMZ after informed discussion, I would document why PCV was unsuitable and use a defined institutional RT–TMZ protocol rather than improvising the sequence or cycle count.

Before PCV I would review marrow, liver and kidney function, pulmonary history, constipation and neuropathy; provide antiemetics and a bowel regimen; review procarbazine interactions; and discuss fertility. I would not start a new cycle until counts and organ toxicity recover according to the institutional order set, commonly ANC at least 1.5 × 10⁹/L and platelets at least 100 × 10⁹/L.

**What would lose points:** Recommending vorasidenib because the tumor is IDH-mutant; saying TMZ is equivalent; using TMZ alone; copying the intensified no-cap RTOG 9402 regimen; or ignoring the delayed marrow nadir of lomustine.

### 17. What I should remember for clinic and boards

1. **No IDH mutation plus whole-arm 1p/19q codeletion, no oligodendroglioma.**
2. Oligodendroglioma has grades **2 and 3**, not grade 4.
3. Selected favorable grade 2 patients can undergo observation or receive vorasidenib if RT/chemotherapy can be deferred.
4. Vorasidenib is **40 mg daily**; its major operational toxicity is hepatotoxicity requiring structured monitoring and dose modification.
5. Vorasidenib is not an evidence-based replacement for definitive therapy in grade 3 or clinically aggressive disease.
6. When definitive treatment is needed, **RT plus PCV has the strongest mature survival evidence**.
7. Conventional adjuvant PCV is lomustine day 1, procarbazine days 8–21, and vincristine days 8 and 29, every six weeks for six cycles.
8. TMZ is reasonable when PCV toxicity is a concern, but it is not yet proven equivalent.
9. **TMZ alone is not enough** for a patient who needs definitive treatment.
10. Follow nonenhancing disease on **T2/FLAIR**, image for many years, and interpret early post-RT changes cautiously.
