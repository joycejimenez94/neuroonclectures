### Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson, I should be able to:

1. Recognize the radiographic and clinical pattern of PCNSL without treating MRI as pathology.
2. Protect diagnostic yield by managing corticosteroids correctly before biopsy.
3. Order complete CNS, ocular, CSF, and systemic staging.
4. Explain the WHO-HAEM5 entity and the minimum pathology needed for diagnosis.
5. Distinguish HD-methotrexate eligibility from transplant or MATRix eligibility.

8. Assess response using MRI, corticosteroid dose, CSF, and ocular status.

### 1. What PCNSL is—and is not

Primary CNS lymphoma is lymphoma arising within and initially confined to the **brain, leptomeninges/CSF, spinal cord, cranial nerves, and/or vitreoretinal compartment**, without systemic lymphoma identified at diagnosis. Most cases are aggressive mature B-cell lymphomas.

WHO-HAEM5 places these tumors within **primary large B-cell lymphomas of immune-privileged sites**, an umbrella biologic family that includes CNS, vitreoretinal, and testicular primary sites. The practical diagnostic wording for a brain lesion remains primary large B-cell lymphoma/DLBCL of the CNS, not merely “immune-privileged-site lymphoma.”

PCNSL must be distinguished from:

- Systemic lymphoma with secondary CNS involvement
- Post-transplant or other immunodeficiency-associated CNS lymphoma
- Primary vitreoretinal lymphoma with or without brain disease
- Intravascular large B-cell lymphoma
- Rare primary CNS T-cell lymphoma, lymphoblastic lymphoma, Burkitt lymphoma, or indolent small B-cell lymphoma
- Glioma, tumefactive demyelination, infection, neurosarcoidosis, vasculitis, metastasis, and treatment-related inflammatory lesions

Why the distinction matters: PCNSL is often highly responsive to methotrexate but is not adequately treated with standard systemic DLBCL regimens such as R-CHOP alone, because the key drugs do not reliably achieve therapeutic CNS concentrations.

### 2. Clinical and MRI recognition

#### Common presentations

- Subacute cognitive, behavioral, or personality change
- Focal neurologic deficit
- Gait dysfunction
- Headache or symptoms of increased intracranial pressure
- Seizure, although less commonly than in infiltrating glioma
- Visual floaters, blurred vision, or reduced acuity from vitreoretinal disease
- Cranial neuropathy, radiculopathy, or myelopathy in less common meningeal, nerve, or spinal presentations

#### Typical MRI pattern in an immunocompetent adult

- One or more avidly enhancing lesions
- Deep or periventricular location; corpus callosum, basal ganglia, thalamus, or deep white matter are common
- Restricted diffusion from high cellularity
- Often relatively low perfusion compared with high-grade glioma
- T2/FLAIR abnormality that may extend beyond enhancement
- Homogeneous enhancement and less necrosis than glioblastoma are classic, but neither is obligatory

In immunocompromised patients, multifocality, hemorrhage, and necrosis may be more frequent. EBV association is much stronger in immunodeficiency-associated disease.

**Attending-level rule:** MRI can make PCNSL likely, but it cannot establish the diagnosis. A steroid-responsive enhancing lesion is also not diagnostic; inflammatory and demyelinating disease can respond to steroids.

#### Minimum useful brain MRI

Use gadolinium-enhanced 3D T1-weighted imaging plus T2, FLAIR, diffusion/ADC, susceptibility-sensitive imaging, and precontrast T1. Perfusion is helpful for differential diagnosis. Use thin-section postcontrast imaging and consistent acquisition for response assessment. The IPCG recommends a standardized 1.5-T or 3-T protocol.

### 3. The corticosteroid problem: preserve the biopsy target

Corticosteroids can trigger rapid apoptosis and radiographic disappearance of lymphoma. They may convert a diagnostic specimen into nonspecific inflammation or necrosis.

#### If the patient is neurologically stable

- **Avoid dexamethasone before biopsy.**
- Obtain urgent neurosurgical consultation and biopsy promptly.
- Manage headache or nausea with non-steroid measures when feasible.

#### If there is dangerous edema, impending herniation, or rapid deterioration

- Stabilization takes priority; give the lowest effective corticosteroid dose.
- Arrange biopsy as urgently as possible, ideally while an enhancing target remains.
- Do not withhold life-saving steroids merely to preserve tissue.

#### If steroids were already administered

- If a target remains, expedite biopsy; a mandatory washout interval is not evidence based.
- If the lesion has markedly regressed and the patient is stable, taper/stop steroids when safe and repeat MRI to identify a returning target.
- Coordinate the plan among neuro-oncology, neurosurgery, neuroradiology, and pathology rather than repeatedly sampling a disappearing lesion.

The common error is not “ever using steroids.” The error is starting them reflexively in a stable patient and then delaying tissue acquisition.

### 4. Establishing the diagnosis

#### Preferred procedure

**Stereotactic biopsy of an enhancing lesion** is the standard diagnostic procedure. Send adequate material for:

- Histology
- Immunohistochemistry
- Flow cytometry when feasible
- Cytogenetic/molecular studies when indicated
- Microbiologic studies if infection remains in the differential

Routine gross-total resection has historically not been recommended because PCNSL is often multifocal/infiltrative and highly chemosensitive. Selected patients with a single superficial lesion causing acute mass effect may undergo debulking, but retrospective suggestions of benefit have not replaced biopsy as the general guideline standard.

#### Pathologic pattern

Typical PCNSL is an angiocentric large B-cell lymphoma with large atypical lymphoid cells and a high proliferation index.

Minimum EHA–ESMO immunohistochemical panel:

- CD20 and CD3
- CD10
- BCL6
- BCL2
- MUM1/IRF4
- Ki-67

Additional studies commonly include PAX5, CD79a, MYC, EBER in situ hybridization, and light-chain studies according to the context. Most immunocompetent PCNSL has a nongerminal-center/activated B-cell phenotype. MYD88, commonly L265P, and CD79B alterations support the characteristic B-cell receptor/Toll-like receptor–NF-κB biology, but neither is required and neither alone proves primary CNS origin.

#### Immunocompromised host

Document HIV status and the nature of immunosuppression. EBV testing is important; many HIV- or transplant-associated cases are EBV driven. Treatment requires simultaneous management of the immune state and differs in important ways from routine immunocompetent PCNSL.

#### When brain biopsy is unsafe or nondiagnostic

CSF or vitreous fluid may establish lymphoma if malignant cells are demonstrated. Ancillary CSF/vitreous tests—including flow cytometry, MYD88 L265P, clonal immunoglobulin rearrangement, and IL-10—can support the diagnosis, especially in difficult cases. They should not be used to avoid a safe, feasible brain biopsy merely because the MRI “looks like lymphoma.”

### 5. Complete staging: four compartments plus the rest of the body

Staging has two purposes:

1. Define every involved CNS compartment that must be followed.
2. Exclude systemic lymphoma, which changes the diagnosis and treatment strategy.

#### A. Brain

- Contrast-enhanced MRI with diffusion and standardized measurements
- Repeat after biopsy and ideally within **14 days before treatment**, because PCNSL can change rapidly
- Document each measurable enhancing lesion and corticosteroid dose

#### B. CSF/leptomeninges

Perform lumbar puncture unless contraindicated by mass effect, obstructive hydrocephalus, unsafe intracranial pressure, coagulopathy, or another procedural risk.

Send:

- Opening pressure when clinically relevant
- Cell count/differential, protein, and glucose
- Cytology
- Flow cytometry—ideally on promptly processed, adequate-volume fresh CSF
- Consider MYD88 L265P, IL-10, and/or clonality studies when the diagnosis or compartment involvement remains uncertain

CSF cytology alone has limited sensitivity. A negative result does not exclude PCNSL.

#### C. Eyes

Every patient should have an ophthalmologic examination, even without visual symptoms:

- Dilated fundus examination and slit-lamp assessment by an ophthalmologist experienced in ocular lymphoma
- Optical coherence tomography and retinal imaging when available
- Fluorescein angiography or fundus autofluorescence when indicated
- Vitreous/aqueous sampling if ocular disease is suspected and tissue is needed

Do not equate a routine bedside visual examination with formal ocular staging.

#### D. Spine and peripheral nervous system

- Spine MRI is not routinely required in every asymptomatic patient.
- Obtain contrast-enhanced spine MRI for myelopathy, radiculopathy, cranial/peripheral neuropathy suggesting neurolymphomatosis, or positive CSF.

#### E. Systemic staging

- Whole-body FDG-PET combined with contrast-enhanced CT is preferred when feasible.
- If PET-CT is unavailable: contrast-enhanced CT chest/abdomen/pelvis plus bone marrow aspiration/biopsy and testicular ultrasound.
- Testicular examination is important; ultrasound is particularly relevant if examination or PET is abnormal, PET is unavailable, or clinical suspicion persists.
- If systemic disease is found, reconsider secondary CNS lymphoma or another systemic lymphoma presentation.

EHA–ESMO notes that systemic staging detects extracranial disease in approximately 4%–12% of patients initially presumed to have PCNSL.

### 6. Pretreatment assessment and prognostic scores

#### Required clinical assessment

- Full neurologic examination and KPS/ECOG performance status
- Baseline cognition and function; formal neuropsychological testing when feasible
- Medication and supplement reconciliation
- Frailty, falls, gait, caregiver support, and ability to complete repeated inpatient admissions
- Fertility and reproductive counseling when relevant

#### Laboratory and organ assessment

- CBC with differential and platelets
- CMP: creatinine, electrolytes, bicarbonate, AST, ALT, alkaline phosphatase, bilirubin, albumin
- Calculated creatinine clearance; do not rely on serum creatinine alone in a sarcopenic older adult
- LDH
- HIV antigen/antibody
- Hepatitis B surface antigen, core antibody, and surface antibody before rituximab; hepatitis C testing
- Pregnancy test when relevant
- Urinalysis and baseline urine pH before HD-MTX admission
- ECG and echocardiogram/LVEF when intensive therapy or transplant evaluation requires it

EHA–ESMO uses **creatinine clearance >50 mL/min** and LVEF >45% as general organ-function requirements for HD-MTX-based treatment; many institutional protocols use additional safeguards or higher renal thresholds.

#### IELSG prognostic score

Five adverse factors:

1. Age >60 years
2. ECOG performance status >1
3. Elevated serum LDH
4. Elevated CSF protein
5. Deep brain involvement

This is prognostic, not a rule that determines a single regimen.

#### MSKCC prognostic score

Uses age and KPS:

- Class 1: age ≤50
- Class 2: age >50 and KPS ≥70
- Class 3: age >50 and KPS <70

Again, it estimates outcome; it does not replace assessment of renal function, frailty, or reversibility of disease-related disability.

### 7. Two distinct fitness questions

#### Question 1: Can the patient safely receive HD-MTX?

Consider:

- Measured/estimated creatinine clearance and trend
- Acute kidney injury or chronic kidney disease
- Hydration tolerance and cardiac function
- Third-space fluid such as pleural effusion or ascites, which can delay clearance
- Active infection, severe mucositis, hepatic dysfunction, cytopenias, or poor oral intake
- Interacting medications and recent iodinated contrast
- Ability to remain hospitalized until clearance and follow rescue instructions

#### Question 2: Can the patient tolerate intensive multiagent therapy and ASCT?

Consider:

- Premorbid—not merely presenting—performance status
- Age in the context of physiologic reserve
- Marrow, hepatic, pulmonary, cardiac, and renal function
- Infection risk
- Baseline cognition and neuropathy
- Social support and treatment goals

An older patient may be HD-MTX eligible but not MATRix/ASCT eligible. Conversely, a chronologically younger patient with poor renal clearance may not be HD-MTX eligible.

Poor KPS caused by lymphoma can improve quickly and should not automatically be treated as fixed frailty.

### 8. Principles of induction treatment

**HD-MTX is the backbone.** Guideline-supported regimens generally use at least **3 g/m² infused over 2–4 hours** to achieve therapeutic CNS exposure. CHOP or R-CHOP alone is inadequate for isolated PCNSL.

Induction choice should be linked to the intended consolidation plan from the beginning. Discuss the patient early with hematology/transplant and radiation oncology rather than waiting until the final methotrexate dose.

| Patient setting | Reasonable induction approach | Evidence/status |
|---|---|---|
| Young/fit, transplant-intended | MATRix for trial-matched patients; R–MPV is another established approach, especially in North American practice | MATRix randomized phase 2 evidence; R–MPV prospective phase 2 and randomized RTOG 1114 platform |
| Fit but less suitable for highly intensive cytarabine/thiotepa | R–MPV or MT–R | Prospective multicenter evidence; regimen choice and consolidation vary |
| Older but HD-MTX eligible | HD-MTX-based combination such as R–MP or MT; R–MPV/modified approaches in selected patients | Prospective older-adult studies; no single universally superior regimen |
| HD-MTX ineligible because of nonreversible organ dysfunction/frailty | Individualized non-MTX therapy, radiation, corticosteroid palliation, or supportive care | Inferior evidence and outcomes; specialist/clinical-trial decision |

Rituximab is widely used but remains off-label for PCNSL. Randomized data on its incremental value are mixed: HOVON 105 did not demonstrate a clear overall EFS benefit, while longer IELSG32 follow-up and meta-analysis support a PFS signal with uncertainty.

Routine intrathecal chemotherapy is **not required** when adequate systemic HD-MTX can be delivered. Consider compartment-directed therapy for persistent isolated CSF or ocular disease rather than adding it automatically to every regimen.

### 9. Exact induction regimens—do not blend them

#### A. Classic 14-day R–MPV schedule

The long-term multicenter phase 2 regimen used:

| Drug | Dose and timing |
|---|---|
| Rituximab | **500 mg/m² IV day 1** |
| Methotrexate | **3.5 g/m² IV over 2 hours day 2** |
| Vincristine | **1.4 mg/m² IV day 2**, institutional maximum applies |
| Procarbazine | **100 mg/m² orally daily days 1–7, odd-numbered cycles only** |
| Cycle | Every **14 days**; five initial cycles, then response-adapted additional cycles in the original study |

Leucovorin, hydration, alkalinization, and growth-factor support are administered according to the protocol/institution. Procarbazine interaction counseling and constipation/neuropathy monitoring are essential.

**Modern RTOG 1114 scheduling was different:** four 28-day cycles containing two MTX/rituximab/vincristine administrations per cycle; procarbazine days 2–8; vincristine was stopped in later cycles. Use the actual institutional protocol—do not combine the 14-day and 28-day calendars.

#### B. MT–R: CALGB 50202

| Drug | Dose and timing |
|---|---|
| Methotrexate | **8 g/m² IV over 4 hours every 2 weeks for seven doses**, adjusted for creatinine clearance |
| Rituximab | **375 mg/m² IV weekly for six doses**, beginning on day +3 |
| Temozolomide | **150 mg/m² orally daily on days 7–11 of each of the first five 28-day months** |

No routine intrathecal chemotherapy was used. The original study then used intensive etoposide/cytarabine consolidation; induction cannot be interpreted independently of that design.

#### C. MATRix: IELSG32/IELSG43 platform

Four 21-day courses:

| Drug | Dose and timing in the trial |
|---|---|
| Rituximab | **375 mg/m² IV on days –5 and 0** |
| Methotrexate | **3.5 g/m² IV day 1** |
| Cytarabine | **2 g/m² IV every 12 hours on days 2 and 3**—four total doses |
| Thiotepa | **30 mg/m² IV day 4** |

This is intensive therapy. It requires protocol-defined antimicrobial prophylaxis, growth factor, transfusion support, cytarabine eye prophylaxis, neurotoxicity surveillance, and experienced inpatient care.

EHA–ESMO considers MATRix appropriate for trial-matched patients aged ≤65 with ECOG 0–3 or aged 66–70 with ECOG ≤2. Real-world extension outside these boundaries has produced substantial dose reduction and treatment-related mortality; “fit-looking” is not enough.

#### D. PRIMAIN R–MP approach for older patients

The safer amended PRIMAIN regimen omitted lomustine after infectious toxicity:

| Drug | Dose and timing |
|---|---|
| Rituximab | **375 mg/m² IV days 1, 15, and 29** |
| Methotrexate | **3 g/m² IV over 4 hours days 2, 16, and 30** |
| Procarbazine | **60 mg/m² orally days 2–11** |
| Course | 42-day course; three courses were planned in the study |

The original R–MPL cohort also received lomustine 110 mg/m² day 2, but lomustine was removed because of infectious complications. Do not reinsert it casually.

**Key older-adult principle:** age alone does not justify lowering MTX when renal function is preserved. EHA–ESMO states that MTX ≥3 g/m² is tolerable in many older adults and that dose reduction should be driven by renal function and comorbidity, not chronological age alone.

### 10. HD-MTX: a medication system, not merely a dose

Safe HD-MTX requires all of the following:

1. Pre-treatment renal and medication assessment
2. Prehydration
3. Urinary alkalinization
4. Timed infusion
5. Timed leucovorin rescue
6. Serial MTX concentrations and creatinine
7. Continued hydration/alkalinization until clearance
8. A predefined delayed-clearance and glucarpidase pathway

#### Before every dose

- CBC with differential and platelets
- Creatinine and calculated creatinine clearance; compare with the patient’s own baseline
- Electrolytes, bicarbonate, AST/ALT, bilirubin, albumin
- Weight, intake/output, edema and cardiopulmonary examination
- Urine pH and ability to maintain urine output
- Review infection, mucositis, diarrhea/vomiting, effusions/ascites, and recent contrast
- Reconcile all prescription drugs, OTC medications, and supplements

Do not proceed reflexively through new AKI, dehydration, uncontrolled infection, clinically important third spacing, or an interacting drug that cannot be safely managed.

#### Important interacting medications

Commonly held before and during HD-MTX until clearance, using institutional timing:

- NSAIDs and high-dose aspirin
- Proton-pump inhibitors
- Trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole and other folate antagonists
- Penicillins and selected other antibiotics that reduce tubular secretion
- Probenecid
- Other nephrotoxins
- Iodinated contrast when avoidable near treatment

Also review anticonvulsants and supplements with oncology pharmacy. Do not stop a necessary antiseizure medication without providing a safe alternative.

#### Hydration and alkalinization targets

- Start bicarbonate-containing IV hydration before MTX according to the institutional protocol.
- Document **urine pH ≥7 before infusion** and maintain it during clearance.
- Maintain urine flow approximately **≥2.5 L/m²/day**; falling below 2 L/m²/day increases delayed-clearance risk.
- Check urine pH frequently and give additional bicarbonate per protocol.
- Acetazolamide may be used for inadequate urine alkalinization or problematic systemic alkalosis under protocol guidance.
- Continue hydration and alkalinization until MTX reaches the local safe-discharge threshold and renal function is stable.

#### Leucovorin rescue

Leucovorin is mandatory. A common starting approach is **15–25 mg orally or IV every 6 hours beginning 24 hours after the start of MTX**, but the starting dose, route, and exact time are regimen specific.

After initiation, leucovorin must be adjusted to the measured MTX concentration and renal function. High MTX concentrations require much higher IV leucovorin doses; do not continue a fixed low dose while clearance is delayed.

Continue until MTX is below the institutional threshold—commonly **<0.1 μmol/L**, sometimes <0.05 or <0.2—and the patient is clinically stable with recovering renal function.

#### Laboratory monitoring

- MTX concentration at protocol-defined times, commonly 24, 48, and 72 hours from infusion start, then daily until cleared
- Serum creatinine at least daily; more often with delayed elimination
- Electrolytes, bicarbonate, fluid balance, weight, urine output, and urine pH
- CBC and hepatic tests because myelosuppression, mucositis, and transaminitis may follow delayed exposure

The absolute expected 24-hour concentration depends on dose and infusion duration. Interpret the level against the regimen’s pharmacokinetic curve rather than memorizing one universal “normal” value.

### 11. Delayed clearance and glucarpidase

Suspect delayed elimination when MTX concentrations remain above the expected curve, creatinine rises, urine output falls, or fluid/electrolyte abnormalities develop.

#### Immediate response

- Stop interacting/nephrotoxic drugs.
- Increase hydration and maintain urine pH ≥7 while avoiding dangerous fluid overload.
- Escalate leucovorin according to the MTX level and protocol.
- Recheck the level if contamination from the MTX infusion lumen is possible.
- Involve oncology pharmacy and nephrology early.
- Evaluate for effusions, obstruction, infection, and other causes of AKI.

#### When to consider glucarpidase

For a short HD-MTX infusion with rising creatinine/AKI, consensus thresholds include:

- 24-hour MTX >50 μmol/L
- 36-hour MTX >30 μmol/L
- 42-hour MTX >10 μmol/L
- 48-hour MTX >5 μmol/L

These thresholds must be interpreted in relation to dose, infusion duration, renal change, and the expected pharmacokinetic curve.

**Dose:** glucarpidase **50 units/kg IV once over approximately 5 minutes**.

Give as early as possible, ideally within **48–60 hours from the start of MTX**, when indicated. It rapidly removes circulating MTX but not intracellular MTX, so leucovorin remains essential.

Hold leucovorin for at least **2 hours before and 2 hours after glucarpidase** because glucarpidase also cleaves leucovorin. Routine immunoassays overestimate MTX after glucarpidase because they detect DAMPA; use the institutional post-glucarpidase assay/interpretation pathway.

Dialysis is not the preferred substitute when timely glucarpidase is indicated, but high-flux dialysis may be considered with nephrology for severe renal failure/anuria when glucarpidase is unavailable or additional removal is required.

### 12. Regimen-specific supportive care

#### Rituximab

- Screen for hepatitis B before treatment.
- HBsAg-positive or anti-HBc-positive patients require specialist antiviral risk management; reactivation can be fatal.
- Premedicate for infusion reactions per protocol.
- Monitor immunoglobulins and infections during prolonged therapy when clinically indicated.

#### Procarbazine

- Review serotonergic drugs, sympathomimetics, alcohol, and tyramine-containing foods with pharmacy because of monoamine-oxidase-inhibiting properties.
- Provide antiemetics.
- Monitor marrow and hepatic toxicity.
- Counsel regarding fertility and pregnancy prevention.

#### Vincristine

- Baseline and serial neuropathy assessment.
- Active constipation prophylaxis.
- Reduce or omit for clinically important neuropathy or ileus according to protocol.
- **Never administer intrathecally.**

#### High-dose cytarabine

- Steroid eye drops beginning before treatment and continuing after the final dose according to protocol.
- Cerebellar examination before each dose; hold and evaluate new dysarthria, nystagmus, ataxia, or confusion.
- Renal and age-based dose modification is protocol dependent.
- Expect profound cytopenias; use growth factor, transfusion, and antimicrobial prophylaxis according to the regimen.

#### Thiotepa/intensive therapy

- Requires experienced hematology care, antimicrobial prophylaxis, growth factor, and close hepatic/marrow monitoring.
- Skin excretion can cause toxicity; follow protocol-specific bathing and linen precautions.

#### Infection prophylaxis

The need depends on regimen intensity, corticosteroid exposure, neutropenia, and lymphopenia. MATRix generally requires broader prophylaxis than HD-MTX alone.

- Consider PJP prophylaxis; TMP–SMX must be coordinated around MTX because of interaction, and atovaquone or inhaled pentamidine may be preferable during intensive MTX scheduling.
- Use HSV/VZV and antifungal prophylaxis according to regimen and institutional risk assessment.
- Use G-CSF when specified by the regimen or for secondary prophylaxis.

### 13. Dose holds, reductions, and contraindications

There is no single universal dose-modification table across all PCNSL protocols. Use the actual regimen’s order set.

#### Methotrexate

- Hold for active AKI or inadequate clearance from the preceding dose.
- Recalculate creatinine clearance before every dose.
- EHA–ESMO supports renal-function-based reduction when eGFR/CrCl is <50 mL/min; whether treatment can proceed below this level is a specialist, protocol-specific decision.
- Hold for clinically important dehydration, uncontrolled infection, severe mucositis, or problematic third-space fluid until corrected.
- Resume only after MTX has cleared and organ toxicities have recovered to the protocol requirement.
- Prior delayed clearance requires an individualized next-dose plan; do not automatically repeat the same dose.

#### Cytarabine

- Hold for new cerebellar toxicity.
- Modify for renal dysfunction and older age according to the specific regimen because neurotoxicity risk rises.
- Delay for inadequate count recovery or uncontrolled infection per protocol.

#### Vincristine

- Reduce/omit for neuropathy, ileus, or significant hepatic dysfunction.

#### Procarbazine/temozolomide/thiotepa

- Delay or reduce for cytopenias, significant hepatic toxicity, or other grade 3–4 treatment-related toxicity according to the named protocol.

#### Absolute or near-absolute barriers to standard HD-MTX delivery

- Pregnancy unless a highly specialized risk–benefit decision supports treatment
- Inability to deliver rescue or monitor drug levels
- Uncontrolled severe renal failure/AKI without a viable protocol
- Severe hypersensitivity to MTX
- A care environment without alkalinization, serial MTX assays, leucovorin escalation, and emergency access to glucarpidase

### 14. Response assessment during induction

EHA–ESMO recommends gadolinium-enhanced brain MRI **every two courses during induction** and approximately **2 months after consolidation**, compared with the pretreatment baseline. Reassess ocular and CSF disease if involved initially.

IPCG response is multi-compartmental:

| Category | Brain MRI | Steroids | Eye | CSF |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Complete response | No enhancing disease | Off steroids | No active disease | Negative |
| CR unconfirmed | Tiny residual enhancement attributable to biopsy/hemorrhage or minor qualifying uncertainty | Minimal/none per criteria | No active disease | Negative |
| Partial response | ≥50% decrease in enhancing tumor burden | Stable/decreasing | Must not show progression | Must not show progression |
| Stable disease | Does not meet PR or PD | Consider clinical context | No progression | No progression |
| Progressive disease | >25% increase from best response, new lesion, or new/recurrent ocular/CSF/systemic disease | Steroid dose does not rescue the classification | May define PD | May define PD |

Do not call a patient in CR while they still require corticosteroids to suppress enhancement. Also remember that treatment-related enhancement, biopsy change, infarct, and infection can mimic residual tumor.

FDG-PET and advanced MRI can add biologic information, but standard IPCG response remains anchored to contrast-enhanced MRI plus steroid, ocular, and CSF status.

### 15. What the pivotal induction studies proved

#### IELSG20: why methotrexate alone is not always enough

Randomized phase 2 comparison of HD-MTX 3.5 g/m² alone versus HD-MTX plus cytarabine 2 g/m² every 12 hours for two days.

- ORR: **40% versus 69%** favoring the combination.
- Three-year PFS: **21% versus 38%**.
- Three-year OS: **32% versus 46%**, a favorable trend.

**Limitation:** Phase 2 size and older treatment era; consolidation and supportive care influence outcome. It established cytarabine as an active induction component.

#### IELSG32: why MATRix became a standard intensive option

International randomized phase 2 trial in 227 eligible, HIV-negative patients aged 18–70. It compared MTX–cytarabine, the same plus rituximab, and MATRix with thiotepa.

- Primary endpoint: complete remission rate.
- MATRix CR rate in the original report: **49%**, versus 30% with MTX–cytarabine–rituximab and 23% with MTX–cytarabine.
- At 88-month follow-up, seven-year PFS: **52% versus 29% versus 20%**.
- Seven-year OS: **56% versus 37% versus 26%**.

**Limitations:** Phase 2, highly selected fit population, and outcomes depend on subsequent consolidation. Treatment-related deaths occurred; extrapolation to frail or substantially older patients is unsafe.

#### R–MPV program

Prospective studies established high response rates with R–MPV followed by reduced-dose WBRT and cytarabine or by transplant-based consolidation. RTOG 1114 subsequently randomized R–MPV–A with versus without 23.4-Gy WBRT:

- Complete response: 92.3% with chemoradiotherapy versus 76.3% with chemotherapy.
- Median PFS: not reached versus 2.1 years; HR 0.47.
- Two-year PFS: **78.7% versus 54.0%**.
- OS difference was not statistically significant at reported follow-up.

**Interpretation:** Reduced-dose WBRT improved disease control on this platform, but long-term neurocognitive follow-up and comparison with transplant strategies matter. This is primarily a consolidation question and will be revisited in Lesson 10.

#### CALGB 50202: MT–R without upfront WBRT

Multicenter phase 2 study using MT–R induction followed by intensive etoposide/cytarabine consolidation. It demonstrated that a cooperative-group chemotherapy-only program could produce durable control without routine upfront WBRT.

**Limitations:** Single-arm design and intensive consolidation; it does not prove that MT–R induction alone is sufficient.

#### PRIMAIN: older patients can still receive active therapy

Prospective multicenter study in patients ≥65. The amended R–MP regimen was more feasible than R–MPL after lomustine-related infectious toxicity. Across the study, two-year PFS was approximately 37% and OS 47%.

**Limitation:** Single-arm, heterogeneous initial/amended regimen, and substantial toxicity. It supports HD-MTX-based treatment in selected older adults, not one-size-fits-all dosing.

#### New in 2026: MATRix/IELSG43

This phase 3 trial does not change the induction doses—everyone received four MATRix cycles—but it changes how a fit patient should be counseled before induction because consolidation planning is now more evidence based. Among 229 randomized responders, three-year PFS was **78% with carmustine–thiotepa ASCT versus 51% with R–DeVIC**, HR 0.43; both PFS and OS favored ASCT.

**Practical implication:** If using MATRix in a fit patient, transplant evaluation and stem-cell planning are integral to the treatment pathway, not an afterthought. Full consolidation details belong in Lesson 10.

### 16. Attending-style clinical case 1

#### Case

A 62-year-old woman presents with two weeks of confusion and left-sided weakness. MRI shows a 2.8-cm homogeneously enhancing right periventricular lesion with marked diffusion restriction and a second small corpus-callosum lesion. She is stable on examination and has not received corticosteroids. KPS is 70 because of the current neurologic deficits. Creatinine clearance is 78 mL/min, LVEF 60%, and CBC/hepatic tests are adequate.

#### Model reasoning

My leading diagnosis is PCNSL, but MRI is not diagnostic. Because she is stable, I would avoid dexamethasone and expedite stereotactic biopsy of an enhancing target. I would ask pathology to evaluate morphology plus CD20, CD3, CD10, BCL6, BCL2, MUM1, Ki-67 and appropriate ancillary studies, including EBER and flow/molecular testing as indicated.

After diagnosis, I would complete staging with a treatment-baseline brain MRI, CSF cytology and flow if LP is safe, formal slit-lamp/dilated ocular evaluation, and whole-body FDG-PET/CT. I would obtain HIV, LDH, hepatitis B/C studies, CBC/CMP, measured or carefully calculated creatinine clearance, and baseline cognitive/functional assessment.

She appears HD-MTX eligible and potentially transplant eligible. Her presenting KPS may improve with lymphoma treatment and should not by itself exclude intensive therapy. Reasonable induction choices include MATRix if she meets full intensive-treatment criteria and the center intends thiotepa-based ASCT, or an R–MPV-based program with a defined consolidation plan. I would involve the transplant team before cycle 1.

For any HD-MTX dose, I would hold interacting medications, prehydrate, ensure urine pH ≥7, start timed leucovorin, and monitor MTX and creatinine until clearance. I would not say “we can decide about consolidation later,” because the regimen choice, stem-cell planning, and toxicity discussion are connected.

**What would lose points:** Starting dexamethasone automatically before biopsy; diagnosing PCNSL from MRI; ordering R-CHOP alone; using serum creatinine without creatinine clearance; or selecting MATRix without assessing transplant intent and physiologic fitness.

### 17. Attending-style clinical case 2

#### Case

A 78-year-old man has biopsy-proven primary large B-cell lymphoma of the CNS. He lives independently but is now KPS 60 from gait dysfunction and cognitive slowing. CrCl is 54 mL/min, LVEF 55%, and liver function and counts are adequate. PET-CT shows no systemic disease; slit-lamp examination and CSF are negative. He has been taking omeprazole, ibuprofen, and trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole.

#### Model reasoning

I would separate HD-MTX eligibility from transplant/MATRix eligibility. At 78 with borderline renal clearance, he is not a routine MATRix/ASCT candidate, but age alone does not make methotrexate inappropriate. His poor KPS may be disease driven and reversible.

I would stop or replace interacting medications with pharmacy guidance—particularly the PPI, NSAID, and TMP–SMX—and repeat renal assessment after hydration. If CrCl remains just above 50 and he can tolerate inpatient alkalinization and monitoring, an older-adult HD-MTX-based regimen such as R–MP is defensible, using the institutional renal-adjustment algorithm. I would not simply order full-intensity MATRix, nor would I reduce MTX solely because he is 78 without considering clearance and treatment goals.

I would explain that the PRIMAIN R–MP platform used rituximab 375 mg/m² on days 1, 15, and 29; MTX 3 g/m² on days 2, 16, and 30; and procarbazine 60 mg/m² on days 2–11 of a 42-day course. However, his CrCl is near the lower boundary and the actual dose must follow the local protocol. If renal function worsens below a safely treatable level or hydration is not tolerable, I would reassess non-MTX options and goals rather than forcing treatment.

During admission I would require urine pH ≥7, adequate urine flow, timed leucovorin and serial MTX/creatinine. A creatinine rise plus an MTX level above the expected curve would trigger immediate intensified rescue and early glucarpidase consideration—not “wait until tomorrow.”

**What would lose points:** Declaring all patients over 70 ineligible for MTX; giving unadjusted MTX despite worsening renal function; overlooking the three interacting medications; or treating a serum creatinine that looks “normal” as proof of normal clearance.

### 19. What I should remember for clinic and boards

1. **Do not give steroids before biopsy unless the patient clinically needs them.**
2. Diagnosis requires tissue whenever safely feasible; MRI response to steroids is not pathology.
3. Stage the brain, CSF, eyes, relevant spine/nerve compartments, and the whole body.
4. Check creatinine clearance—not only creatinine—before every HD-MTX dose.
5. HD-MTX eligibility is not the same as MATRix or transplant eligibility.
6. Use at least 3 g/m² over 2–4 hours for a PCNSL-directed HD-MTX regimen unless a validated protocol specifies otherwise.
7. Never mix named regimens: classic R–MPV, RTOG 1114 R–MPV, MT–R, MATRix, and PRIMAIN have different calendars.
8. HD-MTX is inseparable from hydration, urine pH ≥7, leucovorin, serial levels, and a glucarpidase plan.
9. A normal-looking creatinine in an older sarcopenic patient can conceal poor clearance.
10. R-CHOP alone is inadequate for isolated PCNSL.
11. Routine intrathecal therapy is unnecessary when adequate systemic HD-MTX is delivered; use compartment-directed treatment selectively.
12. Plan consolidation before induction begins; 2026 phase 3 data strengthen thiotepa-based ASCT for fit MATRix responders.

### Source and safety note

Guideline recommendations, trial regimens, consensus rescue thresholds, and expert-practice distinctions are labeled separately. HD-MTX leucovorin nomograms, renal dose adjustments, count thresholds, and interacting-drug hold periods differ by regimen and institution. An individual order must use the active institutional protocol, real-time MTX assay, and oncology-pharmacy guidance. This educational chapter is not a substitute for patient-specific hematology, neuro-oncology, nephrology, and pharmacy management.
